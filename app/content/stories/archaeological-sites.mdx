  ## Introduction
  There are countless archaeological sites around the world with new discoveries everyday. They reveal invaluable information about human history and culture. However, many of these sites are under frequent threat of destruction, whether it be from war, seismic activity, or weather disasters. Here, we’ll focus on weather-related threats. According to UNESCO, 73% of their World Heritage sites are threatened by water-related hazards such as coastal floods, droughts, storms, and river floods [1]. Using satellite imagery, we can view examples of archeological sites impacted by natural disasters through the scars and water they leave behind on the underlying landscape.
  </Prose>

  # Sudan (2020)
  Ancient African civilizations were mainly situated along bodies of water for fertile soil and water availability. This makes North African archaeological sites especially vulnerable to river flooding. In Sudan, a wet rainy season in the summer and fall of 2020 led to significant flooding and near-record high waters on the Nile River. The floods killed over 100 people and destroyed over 100,000 homes [5].

<Block>
  <Prose>
  Floods threatened several ancient Sudanese archaeological sites, including the Nubian pyramids of Meroe. Two sites from the 2000-year old Kingdom of Kush and the Napatan/Meroitic cultures were damaged. Floodwaters eroded sandstone that increased the risk of future collapse of buildings and deteriorated the color of painted surfaces. These sites were surveyed by UNESCO to work on restoration and mitigation efforts for future floods [6].
  </Prose>

  <Prose>
  # Texas (2019)
  
  In the United States, the footprints of many indigenous tribes persist in the form of ancient mounds. These centuries-old dirt mounds were likely used for religious practices like burials, ceremonies, and temples. On April 13, 2019, an EF3 tornado devastated the Caddo Mounds State Historical site in Weeping Mary, Texas. The tornado struck during Caddo Culture Day celebrations. The Caddo Tribe is an ancient native tribe that inhabited parts of modern-day Texas, Louisiana, Arkansas, and Oklahoma. The Visitor’s Center, which serves as a museum, was demolished. Inside, many staff, members of the Caddo Tribe, and visitors were injured. One visitor lost their life and several more were seriously injured. A carefully built replica grass house was destroyed as well. Fortunately, out of respect for the Caddo people, all artifacts in the museum were replicas, and no historical artifacts were lost, nor were the mounds themselves impacted. In 2024, the Caddo Mounds site was finally reopened after being rebuilt [7].
  </Prose>

  # Conclusion
  The weather threatening archeological sites will evolve as the climate continues to change. A study by The Nature Conservancy found that several archeological sites in California are prone to sea-level rise and erosion in the 21st Century, with dozens actively eroding [4]. This research can be used to prioritize the excavation of more threatened sites. Much of ancient history is buried underground, which is one of the reasons why they are so susceptible to floods and landslides. However, sometimes disasters can reveal new pieces of history. Wildfires frequently unveil new artifacts. In 2006, a fire in California razed the landscape but revealed 2,000 year-old granite tables [2]. In 2025, a fire revealed the buried remains of buildings of an ancient biblical city in Israel [3]. When considering preserving the integrity of archaeological sites, weather will always be a primary consideration to reduce the potential loss of irreplaceable history.
