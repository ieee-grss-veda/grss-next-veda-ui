  ## Introduction
  There are countless archaeological sites around the world with new discoveries everyday. They reveal invaluable information about human history and culture. However, many of these sites are under frequent threat of destruction, whether it be from war, seismic activity, or weather disasters. Here, we’ll focus on weather-related threats. According to UNESCO, 73% of their World Heritage sites are threatened by water-related hazards such as coastal floods, droughts, storms, and river floods [1]. Using satellite imagery, we can view examples of archeological sites impacted by natural disasters through the scars and water they leave behind on the underlying landscape.
  </Prose>

  # Sudan (2020)
  Ancient African civilizations were mainly situated along bodies of water for fertile soil and water availability. This makes North African archaeological sites especially vulnerable to river flooding. In Sudan, a wet rainy season in the summer and fall of 2020 led to significant flooding and near-record high waters on the Nile River. The floods killed over 100 people and destroyed over 100,000 homes [5].

  <Prose>
  Floods threatened several ancient Sudanese archaeological sites, including the Nubian pyramids of Meroe. Two sites from the 2000-year old Kingdom of Kush and the Napatan/Meroitic cultures were damaged. Floodwaters eroded sandstone that increased the risk of future collapse of buildings and deteriorated the color of painted surfaces. These sites were surveyed by UNESCO to work on restoration and mitigation efforts for future floods [6].

  # Texas (2019)
  
  In the United States, the footprints of many indigenous tribes persist in the form of ancient mounds. These centuries-old dirt mounds were likely used for religious practices like burials, ceremonies, and temples. On April 13, 2019, an EF3 tornado devastated the Caddo Mounds State Historical site in Weeping Mary, Texas. The tornado struck during Caddo Culture Day celebrations. The Caddo Tribe is an ancient native tribe that inhabited parts of modern-day Texas, Louisiana, Arkansas, and Oklahoma. The Visitor’s Center, which serves as a museum, was demolished. Inside, many staff, members of the Caddo Tribe, and visitors were injured. One visitor lost their life and several more were seriously injured. A carefully built replica grass house was destroyed as well. Fortunately, out of respect for the Caddo people, all artifacts in the museum were replicas, and no historical artifacts were lost, nor were the mounds themselves impacted. In 2024, the Caddo Mounds site was finally reopened after being rebuilt [7].
  </Prose>

  ### Sudanese Flooding

  The Quito and Atrato River near Columbia’s Pacific Coast suffers from harmful pollution and coastal habitat loss due to widespread ASGM. The effects of dredging up the rivers can be seen on satellite imagery with the loss of vegetation and the popcorn patterns of exposed sediment and murky water. According to the United Nations, one third of the population in the Atrato River watershed is exposed to unhealthy levels of mercury. Mercury use in Columbian gold mining was banned in 2018, but that hasn’t stopped the illicit practice. The mercury pollution has also been devastating to wildlife such as river dolphins.

  ### Tornado in Texas

  In South America, ASGM has caused nearly 100,000 ha of forest loss over 34 years, with current rates reaching historical highs as mining spreads into small, dispersed “checkerboard” patterns across the landscape. These operations typically involve stripping away vast layers of topsoil and burning local vegetation to access gold deposits. Once the forest is cleared, the landscape is often transformed into permanent, degraded wetlands or bare soil pits with a very slow natural recovery timeline. The severity of the dredged banks of the Rio Quito can be seen through normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), which measures vegetation health. Lower NDVI values indicate surfaces with unhealthy plants or a lack of vegetation. Piles of sediment litter the river surroundings from gold mining operations.

  ### Australian Bush Fires

  Hydrological impacts also arise from the ASGM industry primarily through mercury runoff and the eventual contamination of local waterways by toxic mercury compounds. One of the most prominent of these compounds is methylmercury, an organic mercury compound created by soil- and water-based bacteria which are primarily involved in the process of organic compound remineralization. Methylmercury contamination within fish populations has been linked to decreased fertility within the population, as well as in birds and mammals which consume the fish. Particularly in regions also undergoing deforestation, increasing runoff of both soil and mercury pollutants could contribute to an acceleration of methylmercury waterway contamination, in addition to the increase in suspended sediment which already accompanies a region of elevated soil runoff. Normatized differential turbidity index (NDTI) measures how "dirty" water is with suspended materials. The high NDTI in the Rio Quito indicate significant water pollution.

  <Prose>
  ## Conclusion
  The expansion of ASGM represents a complex challenge where economic necessity frequently conflicts with environmental and public health stability. The industry is now the world's largest source of mercury pollution, releasing toxic elemental mercury that settles into soil and water or enters the atmosphere through the burning of amalgams. This systemic contamination, compounded by the clearing of nearly 100,000 hectares of forest in regions like the Peruvian Amazon, creates a "checkerboard" of degraded land with a natural recovery timeline that can exceed several decades. As mercury transforms into methylmercury and bioaccumulates in dietary staples like fish, local communities face irreversible neurological damage and developmental risks that persist long after mining sites are abandoned.
  
  The integration of advanced remote sensing tools – specifically the HLS dataset – provides a vital pathway for improved governance and monitoring. By leveraging a 2-3 day revisit frequency and 30-meter resolution, stakeholders can detect rapid deforestation and use spectral indices like NDVI and NDTI to track vegetation loss and water turbidity in real-time. These technologies, disseminated through open platforms, empower authorities and communities to visualize the true extent of mining's footprint, facilitating more informed decisions regarding land mitigation and the protection of vulnerable ecosystems.
  </Prose>
